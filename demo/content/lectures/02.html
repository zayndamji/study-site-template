01 Conditioning on an event

Slides s2–4 · Reader §2.1

The idea

Learning that B happened throws away every outcome outside B. What is left is rescaled so that it adds up to one again.

Definition · Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), defined only when P(B) > 0

s2

Read P(A | B) as the probability of A given B. With equally likely outcomes it is a ratio of counts, |A ∩ B| / |B|. s3

One fair die, A = “six”, B = “even”: P(A | B) = (1/6) / (1/2) = 1/3. Of the three even faces left, one is a six. s2

For a fixed B, conditional probability is a probability in its own right, so every rule from Lecture 01 holds with “| B” attached, for example P(A′ | B) = 1 − P(A | B), where A′ is the complement of A. But the bar is not symmetric:

P(A | B) and P(B | A) answer different questions and usually differ

s3
Try it · Conditioning two dice

Pick an event A and the information B. Columns are the first die, rows the second. Shaded squares are the outcomes still possible once you know B; dots mark A. P(A | B) is the share of shaded squares with a filled dot.

The multiplication rule

Multiplying the definition out gives a way to build the probability of “both” from a probability and a conditional probability. s4

Multiplication rule and chain rule

Rearranging the definition: P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B)

s4

Chain rule: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) · P(B | A) · P(C | A ∩ B)

s4

Draws made one after another fit the chain rule exactly: two cards without replacement are both aces with probability (4/52) · (3/51) = 1/221. The second factor is a conditional probability: after one ace is gone, 3 of the 51 remaining cards are aces. s4

Why divide by P(B)?

Once we know B happened, outcomes outside B get probability 0. Inside B nothing tells one outcome apart from another more than before, so their probabilities should keep the same ratios. The only way to keep the ratios and make the total 1 is to divide every probability inside B by P(B); then the part of A that survives has probability P(A ∩ B) / P(B).

Three cards in a row

The chance that three cards dealt without replacement are all hearts is, by the chain rule, (13/52) · (12/51) · (11/50) = 11/850 ≈ 0.0129. Each factor is conditional on the hearts already dealt.

Quick check

Two fair dice are rolled and you learn that the sum is 4. What is the probability that the first die shows 1?

1/3. The sum 4 leaves (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1), three equally likely outcomes, and one of them starts with 1.

02 Independence

Slides s5–6 · Reader §2.2

The idea

Independent events do not inform each other: knowing that B happened leaves the probability of A where it was.

Definition · Independent events

A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)

s5

If P(B) > 0 this says P(A | B) = P(A): learning B changes nothing about A

s5

Independence is a property of the numbers and has to be checked. With two fair dice, “first die shows 6” and “sum is 7” each have probability 6/36, and both happen only for (6, 1): 1/36 = (1/6) · (1/6). They are independent even though the first die is part of the sum. Change the sum to 8 and the product rule fails, 1/36 ≠ (1/6) · (5/36). s5

Pitfall · Disjoint is not independent

Disjoint events with P(A) > 0 and P(B) > 0 are never independent

s6

Disjoint means P(A ∩ B) = 0, while P(A) · P(B) > 0. Far from being unrelated, disjoint events are strongly dependent: Learning that B happened tells you A did not: that is strong dependence. s6

Complements of independent events

If A and B are independent, so are A and B′:

P(A ∩ B′) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B) = P(A) − P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 − P(B)) = P(A)P(B′).

Applying this twice gives the other pairs, A′ with B and A′ with B′. Reader §2.2

Three events: pairwise is not enough

Flip two fair coins. Let A = “first is heads”, B = “second is heads”, C = “the two flips match”. Each has probability 1/2 and each pair meets in one outcome of four, so every pair is independent: 1/4 = 1/2 · 1/2. But all three happen only for HH, so P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 1/4 ≠ 1/8. Any two of the events determine the third. For three or more events, “independent” means the product rule holds for every subcollection.

Quick check

A fair coin is flipped twice. Are “first flip is heads” and “the two flips match” independent?

Yes. Each has probability 1/2, and both happen only for HH, with probability 1/4 = 1/2 · 1/2.

03 Total probability and Bayes’ rule

Slides s7–8 · s11 · Reader §2.3

The idea

To get P(B | A), first work forwards: how likely is each case, and how likely is A in each case? Then divide the part of A that comes from B by all of A.

Law of total probability

A partition: disjoint events B₁, B₂, … that together make up Ω

s7

P(A) = P(A | B₁)P(B₁) + P(A | B₂)P(B₂) + …, one term for each piece

s7

Simplest partition: B and B′, so P(A) = P(A | B)P(B) + P(A | B′)P(B′)

s7
Bayes’ rule

If P(A) > 0,

P(B | A) = P(A | B) · P(B) / P(A) = P(A | B)P(B) / [P(A | B)P(B) + P(A | B′)P(B′)]

Vocabulary: prior P(B), likelihood P(A | B), posterior P(B | A)

s8

Slide s8 prints Bayes’ rule as

P(B | A) = P(A | B) · P(A) / P(B)

s8

with P(A) and P(B) swapped. It should read P(B | A) = P(A | B) · P(B) / P(A): the numerator is P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B) by the multiplication rule s4, and the next bullet on the same slide computes the denominator P(A). The printed version can even exceed 1: with P(A | B) = 1, P(A) = 1/2 and P(B) = 1/4 it gives 2. The reader states the rule correctly. Reader §2.3

Step by step · Deriving Bayes’ rule
  1. Start from the definition, with the roles of the events swapped: P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A), which needs P(A) > 0. s2
  2. Rewrite the numerator with the multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B). s4
  3. Substitute: P(B | A) = P(A | B) · P(B) / P(A). This is Bayes’ rule.
  4. Expand the denominator by total probability over the partition B, B′: P(A) = P(A | B)P(B) + P(A | B′)P(B′). s7
  5. Now everything is a forward probability: P(B | A) = P(A | B)P(B) / [P(A | B)P(B) + P(A | B′)P(B′)].
  6. Check with the test of section 04 (B = sick, A = positive): (0.99 · 0.01) / (0.99 · 0.01 + 0.05 · 0.99) = 0.0099 / 0.0594 = 1/6. s9

The tree-diagram view

Add the paths that end in the same result; Bayes divides one path by that total

s11
Bayes’ rule over a larger partition

With a partition B₁, B₂, … the same derivation gives

P(Bk | A) = P(A | Bk)P(Bk) / ∑j P(A | Bj)P(Bj)

In tree language: one path to A, divided by all paths to A. Monty Hall (Example 4 below) is a three-piece partition in disguise.

The odds form

Dividing Bayes’ rule for B by Bayes’ rule for B′ cancels P(A):

P(B | A) / P(B′ | A) = [P(B) / P(B′)] · [P(A | B) / P(A | B′)]

Posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. For the test of section 04 the prior odds are 1 : 99 and the likelihood ratio is 0.99 / 0.05 = 19.8, so the posterior odds are 19.8 / 99 = 1/5, that is 1 : 5, and the posterior probability is 1/6.

Quick check

A fair coin picks an urn. Urn 1 holds 1 red and 1 blue ball; urn 2 holds 3 red and 1 blue. One ball is drawn. Find P(red) and P(urn 2 | red).

P(red) = 1/2 · 1/2 + 1/2 · 3/4 = 5/8, and P(urn 2 | red) = (1/2 · 3/4) / (5/8) = (3/8) / (5/8) = 3/5.

04 Diagnostic tests and base rates

Slides s9–10 · s12 · Reader §2.4

The idea

A positive result is only as convincing as the base rate allows. When a condition is rare, false positives from the large healthy group can outnumber the true positives.

Definition · The three numbers of a test

Prevalence P(D), sensitivity P(+ | D), false-positive rate P(+ | D′)

s9

The patient wants P(D | +), and that needs all three numbers

s9

With 1% prevalence, 99% sensitivity and 5% false positives, Bayes’ rule gives P(D | +) = 0.0099 / 0.0594 = 1/6 ≈ 16.7%. Counting people makes the reason visible: out of 10,000 people, 99 sick people test positive, and 495 of the 9,900 healthy people do too. s9–10

So 99 of the 594 positives are sick: 99/594 = 1/6

s10
Try it · Bayes calculator

Type each probability as a fraction (1/6), a decimal (0.05) or a percent (5%). The posterior is computed exactly, then shown again as counts in a population of the size you choose.

Pitfall · The base-rate fallacy

Base-rate fallacy: ignoring P(D) and reading P(+ | D) as if it were P(D | +)

s9

“The test is 99% accurate, so a positive means 99% sure” confuses the two conditionals. Try the rare disease and high-risk group presets: the same test gives 16.7% or 83.2% depending only on the base rate. A rare condition means most positives come from the large healthy group. s10

Updating twice

Yesterday’s posterior is today’s prior

s12

If a second test is independent of the first given the true status, a second positive is handled by running Bayes’ rule again with prior 1/6:

P(D | + +) = (1/6 · 0.99) / (1/6 · 0.99 + 5/6 · 0.05) = 99/124 ≈ 79.8%

s12

Example 3 below loads exactly this into the calculator. The assumption matters: if whatever caused a false positive the first time (say, a cross-reacting substance) tends to cause it again, the two results are not conditionally independent and the second positive is worth less.

What does a negative result tell you?

The calculator’s last line is P(D | −). For the slide example it is (0.01 · 0.01) / (0.01 · 0.01 + 0.99 · 0.95) = 1/9406 ≈ 0.011%: a negative result is very reassuring, because both the base rate and the sensitivity point the same way.

Quick check

Keep 1% prevalence and 99% sensitivity but improve the false-positive rate to 1%. What is P(D | +)?

1/2. Out of 10,000: 99 sick positives and 1% · 9,900 = 99 healthy positives, so 99 / 198 = 1/2. Even a 99%/1% test is a coin flip at this base rate.

05 Worked examples

Slides s7–12 · Reader §2.3–2.4

The idea

Every problem below is the same three moves: say exactly what you learned, write how likely that is in each case, and divide.

Method · A conditioning problem

1. Write the information as an event E, worded exactly as it was obtained. 2. Choose the cases: a partition H, H′ (or more pieces). 3. Find the prior P(H) and the likelihoods P(E | H), P(E | H′). 4. Apply Bayes’ rule, or count paths in a tree.

Beyond the slides · Classic puzzles

These puzzles are not on the slides; they apply the rules of s7–12 to situations where intuition is often wrong.

Open a solution, or press Load in calculator to send the example’s three numbers to the Bayes calculator in section 04, which relabels its outputs to match.

Two childrenwording matters
A family has two children; each is a girl or a boy with probability 1/2, independently. (a) You ask “Is at least one of them a girl?” and the answer is yes. What is the probability that both are girls? (b) Instead you ask “Is the older one a girl?” and the answer is yes. Now what?
Show solution

List the four equally likely families by (older, younger): BB, BG, GB, GG.

(a) “At least one girl” leaves BG, GB, GG, so P(GG | at least one girl) = 1/3. As Bayes: prior P(GG) = 1/4, P(yes | GG) = 1, and among the other three families two answer yes, so P(yes | not GG) = 2/3; then (1 · 1/4) / (1 · 1/4 + 2/3 · 3/4) = (1/4) / (3/4) = 1/3.

(b) “The older is a girl” leaves GB, GG, so the answer is 1/2.

The answers differ because the events you condition on differ. If instead you meet one of the two children at random and she is a girl, P(GG | that child is a girl) = (1/4) / (1/2) = 1/2, not 1/3: how the information was obtained is part of the event.

Two draws from an urnwithout replacement
An urn holds 3 red and 2 blue balls. Two are drawn one after the other without replacement. (a) What is the probability that the second ball is red? (b) Given that the second ball is red, what is the probability that the first was red?
Show solution
  1. Partition on the first draw: P(1st red) = 3/5, P(1st blue) = 2/5.
  2. Conditional on the first draw: P(2nd red | 1st red) = 2/4 (two reds among four left) and P(2nd red | 1st blue) = 3/4.
  3. (a) Total probability: P(2nd red) = 3/5 · 2/4 + 2/5 · 3/4 = 3/10 + 3/10 = 3/5, the same as for the first draw. Before you look, every position is equally likely to hold any ball.
  4. (b) Bayes: P(1st red | 2nd red) = (3/10) / (3/5) = 1/2. Learning about the second draw changes the probability for the first, even though the first came earlier: conditioning is about information, not time.
A second test after a positiveupdating
A person tests positive with the test of section 04 (1% prevalence, 99% sensitivity, 5% false positives) and takes the test again. Assume the two results are independent given the person’s true status. If the second test is also positive, what is the probability that the person is sick?
Show solution
  1. After the first positive, P(sick | +) = 1/6. This is the new prior. s12
  2. The likelihoods are unchanged because of the conditional-independence assumption: P(+ | sick) = 0.99, P(+ | healthy) = 0.05.
  3. Bayes: (1/6 · 0.99) / (1/6 · 0.99 + 5/6 · 0.05) = 0.99 / (0.99 + 0.25) = 99/124 ≈ 79.8%.
  4. Check in one step: two positives in a row have likelihoods 0.99² and 0.05², and (0.01 · 0.99²) / (0.01 · 0.99² + 0.99 · 0.05²) = 0.99 / (0.99 + 0.25) = 99/124 again.
  5. In counts: of the 594 people who tested positive once (99 sick, 495 healthy), on average 98.01 sick and 24.75 healthy people test positive again, and 98.01 / 122.76 = 99/124.
Three doorsMonty Hall
A car is behind one of three doors, equally likely; goats are behind the other two. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is, always opens a door other than yours that hides a goat, choosing at random between two such doors when he can. He opens door 3. Should you switch to door 2?
Show solution
  1. Partition by the car’s position: C₁, C₂, C₃, each with prior 1/3.
  2. Likelihood of the evidence E = “host opens door 3”: P(E | C₁) = 1/2 (he may open 2 or 3), P(E | C₂) = 1 (door 3 is his only goat door), P(E | C₃) = 0 (he never shows the car).
  3. Total probability: P(E) = 1/3 · 1/2 + 1/3 · 1 + 1/3 · 0 = 1/2.
  4. Bayes: P(C₁ | E) = (1/6) / (1/2) = 1/3 and P(C₂ | E) = (1/3) / (1/2) = 2/3.
  5. Switching wins with probability 2/3. With H = C₁ against H′ = “car elsewhere”, P(E | H′) = (1/3 · 1 + 1/3 · 0) / (2/3) = 1/2 = P(E | H): the host’s move says nothing about door 1, which is why its probability stays at 1/3.

The rules of the host are essential. A host who opens a random unchosen door, and happens to reveal a goat, gives P(C₁ | E) = 1/2 instead.

Which coin?repeated evidence
A bag holds two fair coins and one coin with heads on both sides. You take a coin at random and flip it twice: heads both times. What is the probability that you hold the two-headed coin?
Show solution

Prior P(two-headed) = 1/3. Likelihoods of HH: 1 for the two-headed coin and 1/4 for a fair one. Bayes:

P(two-headed | HH) = (1 · 1/3) / (1 · 1/3 + 1/4 · 2/3) = (1/3) / (1/2) = 2/3.

In counts: of 1,200 repeats, 400 draw the two-headed coin and all show HH; of the 800 with a fair coin, 200 show HH. So 400 of the 600 HH results are the two-headed coin.

Quick check

Same bag, but three heads in a row. Now what?

(1/3) / (1/3 + 1/8 · 2/3) = (1/3) / (5/12) = 4/5. Each extra head multiplies the odds by 2.
