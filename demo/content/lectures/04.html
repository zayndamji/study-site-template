01 Bernoulli trials and the binomial count

Slides s2–4 · Reader §4.1

The idea

Count successes in n independent tries: every sequence with k successes has the same probability, so the answer is “how many sequences” times “the probability of one”.

Definition · Bernoulli trial, repeated trials

A Bernoulli trial has two outcomes: success with probability p, failure with 1 − p. Its indicator I is 1 on success and 0 on failure, so E[I] = p and Var(I) = p(1 − p).

In repeated trials, the trials are independent and every trial has the same p.

s2
Definition · Binomial distribution

If X is the number of successes in n repeated trials with success probability p, then X ~ Bin(n, p):

P(X = k) = C(n, k) pk (1 − p)n − k, k = 0, 1, …, n.
Try it · Binomial explorer

Pick a preset, or set n, p (a decimal or a fraction such as 1/6) and a cut-off k₀. Bars up to k₀ are solid, the rest faint; the dashed line marks the mean np. Small cases are computed exactly.

Why C(n, k) sequences, and why it adds up to 1

A run of n trials is recorded as a string of S and F. A string with k S’s has probability pk(1 − p)n−k by independence, whatever the order. The event X = k is the disjoint union of all such strings, and choosing one means choosing which k of the n positions hold the S’s: C(n, k) ways.

For n = 3: the strings with two successes are SSF, SFS, FSS, so P(X = 2) = 3p²(1 − p).

Summing over k gives ∑ C(n, k) pk(1 − p)n−k = (p + (1 − p))n = 1 by the binomial theorem.

Pitfall

The formula needs a fixed number of trials, independence and the same p every time. Drawing cards without replacement fails the last two: the chance of a heart on the second draw depends on the first.

Quick check

A fair die is rolled 4 times. What is the probability of exactly one six?

C(4, 1) · (1/6) · (5/6)³ = 4 · 125/1296 = 125/324 ≈ 0.386.

02 Mean and variance of a binomial

Slides s5–6 · Reader §4.2

The idea

Do not sum k · P(X = k): write the count as one indicator per trial and add their means.

Theorem · Mean and variance

If X ~ Bin(n, p) then

E[X] = np, Var(X) = np(1 − p), SD(X) = √(np(1 − p)).

The standard deviation √(np(1 − p)) grows like √n, not like n.

s5
Step by step · The mean np from indicators
  1. Number the trials 1, …, n and let Ij = 1 if trial j is a success, 0 otherwise.
  2. Each success adds exactly 1 to the count, so X = I1 + I2 + ⋯ + In.
  3. Each indicator has mean E[Ij] = 1 · p + 0 · (1 − p) = p.
  4. Linearity of expectation (true even without independence): E[X] = E[I1] + ⋯ + E[In].
  5. So E[X] = p + p + ⋯ + p = np. Ten fair flips: 5 heads on average; 12 die rolls: 2 sixes on average.
The variance, and a direct check for n = 2

For one indicator, I² = I, so Var(I) = E[I²] − (E[I])² = p − p² = p(1 − p). For independent random variables variances add, so Var(X) = n · p(1 − p). This is the step that needs independence; the mean did not.

Direct check for n = 2, with q = 1 − p: E[X] = 0 · q² + 1 · 2pq + 2 · p² = 2p(q + p) = 2p. The sum works, but the indicator argument scales to any n with no algebra.

Each example below loads its n, p and k₀ into the explorer in section 01.

Exactly halfwarm-up
A fair coin is flipped 10 times. Find P(exactly 5 heads) and P(at most 5 heads).
Show solution

X ~ Bin(10, 1/2), and every sequence has probability 1/210 = 1/1024.

P(X = 5) = C(10, 5)/1024 = 252/1024 = 63/256 ≈ 0.246.

P(X ≤ 5) = (1 + 10 + 45 + 120 + 210 + 252)/1024 = 638/1024 = 319/512 ≈ 0.623. By symmetry this is 1/2 + P(X = 5)/2.

Free throwscomplement
A player makes each free throw with probability 0.8, independently. In 5 shots, find P(all 5) and P(at least 4).
Show solution
  1. X ~ Bin(5, 4/5), with mean 4 and variance 5 · 0.8 · 0.2 = 0.8.
  2. P(X = 5) = 0.8⁵ = 1024/3125 = 0.32768.
  3. P(X = 4) = C(5, 4) · 0.8⁴ · 0.2 = 1280/3125 = 0.4096.
  4. P(X ≥ 4) = 2304/3125 = 0.73728. The explorer (cut-off 3) shows the complement P(X ≤ 3) = 821/3125 = 0.26272.
Quality controlmean vs. typical
Each of 20 parts is defective with probability 0.05, independently. Find P(no defective part), P(at most one), and the mean and variance of the number of defects.
Show solution

X ~ Bin(20, 0.05): mean 20 · 0.05 = 1, variance 20 · 0.05 · 0.95 = 0.95.

P(X = 0) = 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.358; P(X = 1) = 20 · 0.05 · 0.95¹⁹ = 0.95¹⁹ ≈ 0.377.

So P(X ≤ 1) ≈ 0.736: even though one defect is expected, about a quarter of batches have two or more.

At least two sixes
A fair die is rolled 12 times. What is the expected number of sixes, and the probability of at least two?
Show solution

X ~ Bin(12, 1/6), so E[X] = 2 and Var(X) = 12 · (1/6) · (5/6) = 5/3.

Use the complement: P(X ≤ 1) = (5/6)¹² + 12 · (1/6) · (5/6)¹¹ = 17 · 5¹¹/6¹² = 830078125/2176782336 ≈ 0.381.

So P(X ≥ 2) ≈ 0.619. Getting at least the mean is likely here, but far from certain.

Quick check

X ~ Bin(400, 1/2). What are its mean and standard deviation?

Mean 200; variance 400 · 1/2 · 1/2 = 100, so the standard deviation is 10. Four times as many flips as 100 only doubles the spread of the count.

03 Waiting for the first success

Slide s7 · Reader §4.2

The idea

The first success at trial k means k − 1 failures, then a success; on average the wait is 1/p trials.

Definition · Geometric distribution

Let T be the number of repeated trials up to and including the first success. Then

P(T = k) = (1 − p)k − 1 p, k = 1, 2, 3, …, E[T] = 1/p, P(T > m) = (1 − p)m.

Memoryless: past failures do not change the wait that is still ahead.

s7
Try it · Geometric waiting time

Set p and a number of trials m. Bars show P(T = k); the dashed line marks the mean 1/p; bars after m are faint.

Why the mean is 1/p, and why the wait is memoryless

Mean, by conditioning on the first trial. With probability p the first trial succeeds and T = 1. Otherwise one trial is used up and, because the trials are independent, the wait starts afresh. So E[T] = 1 + (1 − p) E[T], which gives p E[T] = 1, that is E[T] = 1/p.

Memoryless. P(T > m + j | T > m) = (1 − p)m+j/(1 − p)m = (1 − p)j = P(T > j): having already waited m trials changes nothing about the wait ahead.

Waiting for a pattern: two heads in a row

Waiting for two heads in a row is no longer a wait for one success, because a tail undoes progress. It helps to remember only how much of the pattern the last flips match: nothing, one head, or both heads. Each flip moves between those three states.

Try it · Flip until HH

Writing a, b for the expected number of flips still needed from “none” and from “H” with a fair coin, conditioning on the next flip gives a = 1 + ½b + ½a and b = 1 + ½ · 0 + ½a, so a = 6: six flips on average, not four.

Quick check

Each attempt succeeds with probability 1/4. What is the chance that the first two attempts both fail? What is the mean number of attempts?

P(T > 2) = (3/4)² = 9/16; E[T] = 4.

04 Running averages and the law of large numbers

Slides s8–9 · Reader §4.3

The idea

The running proportion X/n has mean p and a spread that shrinks like 1/√n, so in the long run it stays close to p.

Definition · Running proportion

After n trials the running proportion of successes is X/n = (I1 + ⋯ + In)/n, with E[X/n] = p and Var(X/n) = p(1 − p)/n.

Theorem · The law of large numbers

For every ε > 0,

P(|X/n − p| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞.

In words: the running proportion settles near p.

s9
Try it · Running proportion simulator

Choose a trial, the number of trials N and a tolerance ε, then press New sample for a fresh run. Every run is reproducible from its sample number.

solid: running proportion dashed: true p band: p ± ε dashed: expected proportion after a forced streak

Proof sketch with Chebyshev’s inequality

Chebyshev’s inequality. For any random variable Y with mean μ and any ε > 0, P(|Y − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Y)/ε².

Why: (Y − μ)² is at least ε² on the event |Y − μ| ≥ ε and at least 0 everywhere else, so Var(Y) = E[(Y − μ)²] ≥ ε² · P(|Y − μ| ≥ ε).

The law. Take Y = X/n, with mean p and variance p(1 − p)/n:

P(|X/n − p| ≥ ε) ≤ p(1 − p)/(nε²) ≤ 1/(4nε²) → 0.

The bound is crude: for a fair coin with n = 10 000 and ε = 0.01 it gives 0.25, while the exact probability is about 0.047. For n = 1000, ε = 0.05 it gives 0.1 against an exact value of about 0.0017.

The proportion settles, the count does not

The standard deviation of X − np is √(np(1 − p)): 5 heads for 100 fair flips, 50 for 10 000, 500 for a million. Dividing by n turns these into 0.05, 0.005 and 0.0005. Watch the count gap readout in the simulator: it wanders and often grows, while |X/N − p| shrinks.

Simulating running proportions

A few lines of code flip a simulated coin and track the running proportion. The marked line is the update that the law of large numbers is about. 

      import random

      def running_proportions(n, p):
          heads, out = 0, []
          for i in range(1, n + 1):
              heads += random.random() < p   # True counts as 1
              out.append(heads / i)
          return out

05 Due for a win? Streaks and dilution

Slide s10 · Reader §4.4

The idea

Independent trials have no memory. Long-run proportions come back to p because later trials swamp a streak, not because they make up for it.

Early streaks are not corrected; they are diluted by later trials.

s10
Dilution, in one line

If the first s trials were all successes and the rest are fresh trials, then after n ≥ s trials

E[X] − np = s(1 − p) for every n, E[X/n] = p + s(1 − p)/n → p.

The excess never shrinks; only its share of the total does.

Try it · Dilution calculator

Enter a streak of s successes at the start and the success probability p. The table shows what is expected after n trials in total, exactly.

Pitfall · The gambler’s misconception

“Five heads in a row, so tails is due.” For a fair coin the next flip is heads with probability 1/2, whatever came before. The law of large numbers is about proportions, and it works by dilution; it never asks the coin to balance a count.

When streaks do mean something

The argument assumes the trials really are independent with a fixed p. If a long streak looks too unlikely under that model, the sensible conclusion is that the model may be wrong — the coin is biased, the wheel is tilted, the shooter is tired — not that the future must even things out. Either way, “due” is never the right word.

Quick check

The first 20 flips of a fair coin were all heads. What is the expected proportion of heads after 1020 flips in total?

(20 + 1000/2)/1020 = 520/1020 = 26/51 ≈ 0.510. The 10-head excess is still there; it is just a small share of 1020.
