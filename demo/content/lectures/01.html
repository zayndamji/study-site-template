01 Outcomes, sample spaces and events

Slides s2–4 · Reader §1.1

The idea

Write down every possible outcome first. An event is then just a set of outcomes, and “E happens” means “the outcome landed in E”.

Definition · Sample space and event

An experiment is a process with an uncertain result; each possible result is an outcome. The sample space Ω is the set of all outcomes.

An event is a subset of the sample space.

s3

Events combine like sets: the complement E′ (not E; many books write Ec), the union E ∪ F (at least one happens), the intersection E ∩ F (both happen). E and F are disjoint when E ∩ F = ∅.

Whether a combined event happens depends only on whether E and F happen. One outcome falls into one of four cases:

Pitfall

Do not treat “a one and a six” and “double three” as two equally likely results. With the dice told apart, “a one and a six” is two outcomes, (1, 6) and (6, 1), while “double three” is one. Listing only unordered results gives 21 possibilities that are not equally likely.

The certain and the impossible event

Ω itself is an event (a subset of itself): it always happens, so it is the certain event. The empty set ∅ never happens: the impossible event. In the explorer, “Clear all” gives E = ∅, and pressing E → E′ then gives E = Ω.

Set rules carry over word for word. For example “not (E or F)” is “not E and not F”: (E ∪ F)′ = E′ ∩ F′ (De Morgan’s law). In the last preset, the outcomes outside E ∪ F are exactly those with both dice odd.

An event is a set of outcomes

Build an event one outcome at a time: add outcomes, take them out, ask whether one is in it. A set keeps each outcome once, so adding an outcome twice changes nothing.

02 Equally likely outcomes and the axioms

Slides s5–7 · Reader §1.2

The idea

When every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is the fraction of outcomes it contains. Everything else about probability follows from three short axioms.

Definition · Equally likely outcomes

If Ω is finite and every outcome is equally likely, then P(E) = |E| / |Ω|.

s5

Two dice: six of the 36 pairs have sum 7, so P(sum is 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

Axioms and consequences

Axioms. (1) P(E) ≥ 0 for every event; (2) P(Ω) = 1; (3) if E and F are disjoint, P(E ∪ F) = P(E) + P(F), and likewise for any sequence of pairwise disjoint events.

Consequences. P(E′) = 1 − P(E); P(∅) = 0; P(E) ≤ 1; if E ⊆ F then P(E) ≤ P(F); and

Inclusion–exclusion: P(E ∪ F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E ∩ F).

s7

The explorer above prints both sides of the complement rule and of inclusion–exclusion for whatever events you pick. The two examples below load straight into it.

A six or doublesoverlapping
Roll two dice. What is the probability of at least one six or a double?
Show solution
Step by step · Inclusion–exclusion
  1. E = at least one six. The first die is 6 in 6 outcomes and the second in 6, but (6, 6) is in both lists, so |E| = 6 + 6 − 1 = 11.
  2. F = doubles: |F| = 6.
  3. E ∩ F = {(6, 6)}, so |E ∩ F| = 1.
  4. P(E ∪ F) = 11/36 + 6/36 − 1/36 = 16/36 = 4/9 ≈ 0.444.
  5. Check with the complement: no six leaves the 25 outcomes with both dice in 1–5, of which 5 are doubles, so 20 outcomes are outside E ∪ F and 36 − 20 = 16.
A sum of 7 or doublesdisjoint
What is the probability that two dice show a sum of 7 or a double?
Show solution

A double has an even sum, so it can never total 7: the events are disjoint and axiom 3 applies directly. P = 6/36 + 6/36 = 12/36 = 1/3.

Why the consequences follow from the axioms

Complement rule. E and E′ are disjoint and E ∪ E′ = Ω, so by axioms 3 and 2, P(E) + P(E′) = 1. With E = Ω this gives P(∅) = 0, and since P(E′) ≥ 0 it also gives P(E) ≤ 1.

Monotonicity. If E ⊆ F, split F into the disjoint pieces E and F ∩ E′; then P(F) = P(E) + P(F ∩ E′) ≥ P(E).

Inclusion–exclusion. E ∪ F is the disjoint union of E and F ∩ E′, and F is the disjoint union of E ∩ F and F ∩ E′. Subtracting the two applications of axiom 3 gives P(E ∪ F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E ∩ F).

The counting rule |E| / |Ω| satisfies all three axioms, because sizes of disjoint sets add.

Quick check

With two dice, what is P(sum ≥ 10 or doubles)?

Answer: 6 outcomes have sum ≥ 10, 6 are doubles, and two, (5, 5) and (6, 6), are both: (6 + 6 − 2)/36 = 10/36 = 5/18.

03 The multiplication rule and “at least one”

Slides s8 · s12 · Reader §1.3

The idea

Count a sequence of choices by multiplying the number of options at each step. To count “at least one”, count the opposite, “none”, and subtract.

The multiplication rule

A task is done in k steps, with n₁ ways to do step 1, n₂ ways to do step 2, and so on.

If the number of ways for each step does not depend on the earlier choices,

then the whole task can be done in n₁ · n₂ · n₃ · … ways, one factor per step.

s8

A 4-digit lock code: 10 · 10 · 10 · 10 = 10,000 codes. Four rolls of a die: 6⁴ = 1296 equally likely sequences.

Step by step · At least one six in four rolls
  1. Sample space. An outcome is a sequence of four rolls. Six choices per roll, so by the multiplication rule |Ω| = 6 · 6 · 6 · 6 = 6⁴ = 1296, all equally likely.
  2. The event. A = at least one six. Counting it directly means separate cases: exactly one, two, three or four sixes.
  3. The complement. A′ = no six at all. Now each roll has 5 choices, so |A′| = 5⁴ = 625.
  4. Its probability. P(A′) = 625/1296.
  5. Complement rule. P(A) = 1 − 625/1296 = 671/1296 ≈ 0.518: slightly better than an even bet.

P(at least one six in four rolls) = 1 − P(no six in four rolls).

s12
Pitfall

The answer is not 4 · 1/6 = 2/3. The events “six on roll 1”, …, “six on roll 4” overlap (two rolls can both be sixes), so their probabilities do not add. Adding them would give more than 1 for seven or more rolls, which no probability can be.

Check by counting the cases directly

Choose which rolls are sixes, then fill the others from five faces: exactly one six in 4 · 5³ = 500 ways, two in 6 · 5² = 150, three in 4 · 5 = 20 and four in 1. The total 500 + 150 + 20 + 1 = 671 matches. (The 4, 6, 4 are the numbers of ways to choose the positions, C(4, k), from section 04.)

A gambler’s puzzle

This bet is tied to a well-known 17th-century story: the Chevalier de Méré is said to have won steadily betting on at least one six in four rolls, and lost on what seemed the same bet, at least one double six in 24 rolls of two dice. The second probability is 1 − (35/36)²⁴ ≈ 0.491, just under one half. Questions like this, passed on to Pascal and Fermat, helped start the mathematical study of probability.

Quick check

How many three-letter codes can be made from 26 letters if letters may repeat? If they may not?

Answer: 26³ = 17,576; without repeats 26 · 25 · 24 = 15,600. The second count is allowed by the multiplication rule because the number of letters left (25, then 24) does not depend on which letters were used.

04 Permutations and combinations

Slides s9–11 · Reader §1.3 · Reader §1.4

The idea

Count ordered lists with the multiplication rule; to count unordered selections, divide by the k! orders in which each one can be listed.

Definition · Factorials, permutations, combinations

n! = n · (n − 1) · … · 2 · 1 orders of n distinct objects, with 0! = 1. Ordered selections of k from n: nPk = n! / (n − k)!.

Unordered selections of k from n: nCk = n! / (k! (n − k)!).

s10

Also written C(n, k), “n choose k”. The link between them: nPk = k! · C(n, k).

Try it · Counting calculator

Enter n (0–120) and k. Results are exact (whole-number arithmetic with no rounding); very long ones are also shown in scientific form.

Card hands, worked out

A deck has 4 suits of 13 ranks. After a fair shuffle all C(52, 5) = 2,598,960 five-card hands are equally likely, so probabilities are counts divided by that number.

  • All five cards of one suit (including straight flushes): choose the suit, then 5 of its 13 cards: 4 · C(13, 5) = 4 · 1287 = 5,148 hands, probability 5148/2598960 ≈ 0.00198.
  • All four aces: take all four, C(4, 4) = 1, and any one of the other 48 cards: 48 hands, probability 48/2598960 = 1/54145 ≈ 0.0000185.

The pattern “choose from each group separately, then multiply” is the multiplication rule applied to combinations.

Why divide by k!

Build an ordered list of k from n in two steps: first choose which k objects (C(n, k) ways), then put them in order (k! ways). The multiplication rule gives nPk = C(n, k) · k!, and dividing gives the formula for C(n, k). The committee preset shows it: 720 ordered = 120 committees · 6 orders.

Counting choices with a recursion

Pascal’s rule, C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k), splits a choice on whether the first item is taken. Run it as a recursion and watch the calls branch; notice how often the same smaller count is worked out again.

05 The birthday problem

Uses s8 · s12 · Reader §1.4

The idea

In a group of only 23 people, a shared birthday is more likely than not. The count of pairs, not of people, is what grows quickly.

Shared birthdays

Assume 365 equally likely birthdays (no leap days) and n people with independent birthdays. Then

P(all different) = 365 · 364 · … · (365 − n + 1) / 365nP(at least one shared birthday) = 1 − P(all different) s12

The top counts ordered lists of n different days (365Pn), the bottom all lists of n days: both by the multiplication rule. For n > 365 a shared birthday is certain.

Try it · Birthday problem

Move the slider or type a group size. The curve shows P(shared birthday) for 1–80 people; the dot is your group. The simulation draws random groups from a seeded generator.

━ P(some two people share a birthday) · ╍ P(someone shares the first person’s birthday) · dashed line at ½

Why the number is so small

Our intuition answers a different question: will someone match me? That chance is 1 − (364/365)n − 1, only about 5.9% for 23 people (the dashed curve). But a match between any two people counts, and 23 people form C(23, 2) = 253 pairs. Each pair matches with probability 1/365, so on average 253/365 ≈ 0.69 pairs match. With that many expected matches, it is no surprise that at least one match happens about half the time.

Quick check

How many pairs of people are there in a group of 30?

Answer: C(30, 2) = 30 · 29 / 2 = 435.
