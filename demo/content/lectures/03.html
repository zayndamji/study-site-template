01 Random variables and PMFs

Slides s2–3 · Reader §3.1

The idea

A random variable is a fixed rule that turns each outcome into a number. Asking about its value is asking about an event, so its probabilities come straight from the outcomes.

Definition · Random variable and PMF

A random variable X assigns a number X(ω) to every outcome ω in Ω.

s2

The PMF of a discrete X is p(k) = P(X = k) for each value k.

s3

Each {X = k} is the set of outcomes sent to k. These events split Ω into pieces, so the values of a PMF are never negative and add up to 1.

Roll two fair dice. The 36 ordered outcomes (a, b) are equally likely, and many numbers can be read off each one:

Two dice: X = first die, S = sum, M = maximum, D = first minus second.

s2

The randomness is entirely in which outcome happens. The function S(a, b) = a + b is as fixed as any formula.

Try it · Events of the form {X = k}

Pick a random variable and a value k, or tap a cell to use its value. Rows are the first die, columns the second; each cell shows the variable's value at that outcome, and the highlighted cells form the event.

Same PMF, different random variables

Different random variables can have the same PMF.

s3

The first die X and the second die Y are different functions: at the outcome (2, 5) one is 2 and the other 5. Yet both have the PMF p(k) = 1/6 for k = 1, …, 6. A PMF records how likely each value is, not which outcomes produce it.

A less obvious pair: the minimum N and 7 − M. Both give 1 with probability 11/36, 2 with probability 9/36, and so on down to 6 with probability 1/36; in general P(N = k) = (13 − 2k)/36.

Quick check

How many outcomes are in {M = 4}, and what is P(M = 4)?

Answer. The outcomes with largest value 4 are (4, 1), (4, 2), (4, 3), (4, 4), (1, 4), (2, 4), (3, 4): seven of them, so P(M = 4) = 7/36. In general P(M = k) = (2k − 1)/36.

02 Expected value

Slides s4–6 · Reader §3.2

The idea

The expected value is the probability-weighted average of the values: the point where the bars of the PMF would balance.

Definition · Expected value

E[X] = Σ k · P(X = k): the values of X weighted by their probabilities.

s4

One fair die: E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 7/2.

s4

A die never shows 3.5: E[X] need not be a value that X can take. s4

Rule · Expectation of a function

E[g(X)] = Σ g(k) · P(X = k): no new PMF is needed.

s5
Try it · PMF explorer

Pick a random variable. The bars are its PMF with exact probabilities; the dashed line marks E[X] and the short solid bar spans one standard deviation either side of it (section 05).

Pitfall

Averaging a function is not applying the function to the average.

In general E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X]): here (E[X])² = 49/4, not 91/6.

s5
Why the two formulas for E[X] agree

Equivalently, E[X] = Σ X(ω) · P({ω}) over all outcomes ω.

s4

Sort the outcomes into the groups {X = k}. Inside one group every term is k · P({ω}), and those probabilities add up to P(X = k):

Σω X(ω) P({ω}) = Σk Σω : X(ω) = k k P({ω}) = Σk k P(X = k).

The same regrouping with g(X(ω)) in place of X(ω) proves the rule for E[g(X)]. For the die, E[X²] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6.

Physically, if each bar of the PMF were a weight placed at its value on a ruler, the ruler would balance at E[X].

Fair games

A bet is fair when the expected net gain is 0.

s6

Pay 1 to roll a die; a six returns 6. The net gain is 5 with probability 1/6 and −1 with probability 5/6, so

E[gain] = 5 · 1/6 + (−1) · 5/6 = 5/6 − 5/6 = 0.

Load the fair game preset above: the mean sits at 0, but E[gain²] = 25 · 1/6 + 1 · 5/6 = 5, so the variance is 5 and the standard deviation about 2.24. You lose most rounds and win rarely but big.

Quick check

A ticket costs 1 and pays 10 with probability 1/10 (and nothing otherwise). Is the bet fair?

Answer. The net gain is 9 with probability 1/10 and −1 with probability 9/10, so E[gain] = 9/10 − 9/10 = 0. Yes, it is fair.

03 Joint distributions and independence

Slides s7–8 · Reader §3.3

The idea

A joint PMF is a table of probabilities for pairs of values. Its row and column sums are the marginals, and the variables are independent exactly when every cell is the product of its two marginals.

Definition · Joint and marginal PMFs

The joint PMF of X and Y is p(x, y) = P(X = x and Y = y).

s7

Marginal PMF: P(X = x) = Σ_y p(x, y), summing out the other variable.

s7
Definition · Independent random variables

X and Y are independent when p(x, y) = P(X = x) · P(Y = y) for all x, y.

s8

One failing cell is enough to show dependence:

First die X and maximum M are dependent: P(X = 6, M = 1) = 0.

s8
Try it · A joint PMF in 3D

Drag to rotate (or focus the picture and use the arrow keys; Home resets). Each bar's height is p(x, y) on one scale shared by all presets; dashed squares are cells of probability 0. Choose a cell to compare it with the product of its marginals.

The marginals do not determine the joint PMF

The marginals do not determine the joint PMF.

s7

Compare the presets two dice and X and X. In both, each variable is uniform on 1, …, 6, so the marginal tables are identical. The joint tables could hardly be more different: 36 short bars of 1/36 against six tall bars of 1/6. Independence is a property of the joint PMF, and you cannot check it from the marginals alone.

Rotate the loaded X, fair Y preset to look along the X axis: every row has the same shape and only its height changes. That is what factoring p(x, y) = P(X = x) · P(Y = y) looks like.

Quick check

For the first die X and the maximum M, find P(X = 3, M = 5) and compare it with P(X = 3) · P(M = 5).

Answer. X = 3 and M = 5 happen together only for the outcome (3, 5), so the joint probability is 1/36. The product is 1/6 · 9/36 = 1/24. They differ, which confirms that X and M are dependent.

04 Linearity of expectation

Slides s9–10 · Reader §3.3

The idea

The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, always, whether or not the parts are independent. Break a complicated count into simple 0/1 pieces and add up their probabilities.

Theorem · Linearity of expectation

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] for any random variables X and Y.

s9

E[aX + b] = a · E[X] + b for constants a and b.

s9

Independence is not needed. s9

Step by step · E[sum of two dice] = 7
  1. Name the parts. Let X be the first die and Y the second, so the sum is S = X + Y.
  2. One die: E[X] = 1 · 1/6 + 2 · 1/6 + … + 6 · 1/6 = 21/6 = 7/2.
  3. The second die has the same PMF, so E[Y] = 7/2 as well.
  4. Linearity: E[S] = E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] = 7/2 + 7/2 = 7. The PMF of S was never needed.
  5. Check against the PMF anyway: Σ k · p(k) = (2·1 + 3·2 + 4·3 + 5·4 + 6·5 + 7·6 + 8·5 + 9·4 + 10·3 + 11·2 + 12·1)/36 = 252/36 = 7.
  6. No step used independence. If the second “die” simply copied the first (Y = X), the same steps give E[2X] = 7, which is also 2 · E[X] by the second rule.
Definition · Indicator variable

The indicator of an event A is 1 if A occurs and 0 if it does not.

s10

E[indicator of A] = 1 · P(A) + 0 · P(not A) = P(A).

s10

Write a count as a sum of indicators; its mean is a sum of probabilities. s10

Try it · Fixed points of random shuffles

Each trial shuffles n items uniformly at random and counts the fixed points, the items left in their own place. The solid line is the running average; the dashed line is the exact answer 1. Add trials, or press New sample for a fresh run (each seed always gives the same run).

Proof of linearity

Use the outcome form of the expected value. Since (X + Y)(ω) = X(ω) + Y(ω),

E[X + Y] = Σω (X(ω) + Y(ω)) P({ω}) = Σω X(ω) P({ω}) + Σω Y(ω) P({ω}) = E[X] + E[Y]. E[aX + b] = Σω (a X(ω) + b) P({ω}) = a E[X] + b · Σω P({ω}) = a E[X] + b.

Nothing here mentions how X and Y relate, which is why independence is never needed.

Why a shuffle has 1 fixed point on average

Fixed points of a random permutation of n items: n · 1/n = 1.

s10

Let Ij be the indicator that item j lands in position j. In a uniformly random arrangement, position j is equally likely to receive any of the n items, so E[Ij] = 1/n. The number of fixed points is I1 + … + In, so its mean is n · 1/n = 1.

Beyond the slides · No fixed point at all

The widget also tracks how often a shuffle has no fixed point. For 3 items that probability is exactly 1/3, for 5 items 11/30, for 10 items 16481/44800 ≈ 0.36788, and as n grows it approaches 1/e ≈ 0.3679. Linearity gives the mean easily, but probabilities like this one need much more careful counting.

Quick check

Roll a die 12 times. What is the expected number of sixes?

Answer. Add 12 indicators, one per roll, each with mean 1/6: 12 · 1/6 = 2.

05 Variance and standard deviation

Slides s11–12 · Reader §3.4

The idea

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Means always add; variances add when the variables are independent.

Definition · Variance and standard deviation

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²], where μ = E[X].

s11

SD(X) = √Var(X), in the same units as X.

s11
Rules · Computing variances

Shortcut: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])².

s11

Var(aX + b) = a² · Var(X): shifting does not change the spread.

s12

If X and Y are independent, Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y).

s12
Try it · Means add; variances only sometimes

Each preset builds two random variables X and Y from one roll of two dice and draws the PMF of X + Y. Compare the last two columns of the table.

Pitfall

“Variances add” is not a general rule like linearity of expectation. It needs independence (or at least zero covariance), and without it the answer can be bigger or smaller.

Without independence it can fail: Var(X + X) = 4 · Var(X).

s12
The variance of one die, step by step
Step by step · Var(die) = 35/12
  1. The mean: μ = E[X] = 7/2.
  2. The mean square: E[X²] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6.
  3. Shortcut: Var(X) = 91/6 − (7/2)² = 182/12 − 147/12 = 35/12 ≈ 2.917.
  4. Standard deviation: SD(X) = √(35/12) ≈ 1.708, in pips, like the die itself.
  5. Check with the definition: the squared distances from 7/2 are 25/4, 9/4, 1/4, 1/4, 9/4, 25/4, and their average is (70/4)/6 = 35/12.
Why the rules hold

Shortcut. Expand the square and use linearity, with μ a constant: E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − 2μ E[X] + μ² = E[X²] − μ².

Scaling. aX + b has mean aμ + b, so its distance from its mean is a(X − μ), and squaring brings out a². The shift b cancels.

Beyond the slides · Covariance

For any X and Y, expanding the square in the same way gives

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y), Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X] E[Y].

When X and Y are independent, the joint PMF factors, so E[XY] = Σx,y xy P(X = x) P(Y = y) = E[X] E[Y] and the covariance is 0. For the first die and the maximum, Cov(X, M) = 35/24, which is why the X + max preset shows 10115/1296 ≈ 7.80 instead of 6335/1296 ≈ 4.89.

Quick check

For one fair die X, what are E[3X + 2] and Var(3X + 2)?

Answer. E[3X + 2] = 3 · 7/2 + 2 = 25/2 and Var(3X + 2) = 9 · 35/12 = 105/4. The +2 moves the mean but not the spread.
