Before you calculate

  1. What is the experiment? Describe one run of it in a sentence: “roll two dice”, “draw three cards without replacement”.
  2. What is one outcome? Write two or three outcomes explicitly. Decide whether order matters and whether repeats are allowed.
  3. Are the outcomes equally likely? Only then may you count: P(E) = |E| / |Ω|. The sums of two dice are not equally likely; the 36 ordered pairs are.
  4. Which event is asked for, exactly? Translate “at least one”, “exactly one”, “given that” into set language before computing.

While you calculate

  1. Is the complement easier? “At least one” is usually 1 − P(none).
  2. Are you multiplying probabilities? Then you are assuming independence, or you need conditional probabilities: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B | A).
  3. Are you adding probabilities? Then the events must not overlap, or you must subtract the overlap.
  4. Is there a base rate? For “given a positive test…” questions, write down how common the condition is before anything else.
  5. Can linearity help? An expected count is a sum of indicator variables, whether or not they are independent.
Pitfall

P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers. Swapping them is the most common error in the subject.

After you calculate

  1. Is the answer between 0 and 1? An expected value need not be, but a probability must.
  2. Does an extreme case agree? Set a parameter to 0 or 1, or the group size to 1, and check the formula gives the obvious answer.
  3. Does a simulation agree? Most widgets in these notes can sample; a few hundred runs catch most mistakes.
  4. Would you bet on it? If the number surprises you, find the step where your intuition and the calculation part ways — that step is the lesson.
